01 — IT STARTS WITH A PROBLEM

Before any truck moves,
someone notices something.

A road is crumbling. Land needs clearing. A building needs a foundation. Whoever has that problem is called the Owner — they're the one who ultimately funds everything that follows.

🛣️

A road is failing

Potholes, cracking, crumbling edges. A city or county decides it needs to be repaved. They fund the whole project.

Public owner
🏗️

Land needs preparing

Empty land must be cleared, leveled, and graded before a building, parking lot, or subdivision can go up.

Developer
🌉

Infrastructure to build

A highway, bridge approach, or utility corridor needs tons of aggregate and fill placed precisely.

State / DOT
🏢

A private project

A factory expansion, warehouse foundation, commercial site — anyone building anything on land.

Private owner
💡  The owner can't build it themselves — so they put the work out for bid and hire professionals to execute. That's what sets everything else in motion.

02 — FROM PROBLEM TO BID

How a job gets created

The owner hires professionals and puts the work out to bid. A bid is a contractor's formal written price offer to do the work — steps 4 and 5 are what goes into pricing it.

1
🏛️

Engineer designs it

An engineer produces full plans and specs: exactly what materials are needed, what depths, what quantities, and what standards the work must meet.

2
📋

Project split into scopes

Large jobs are broken into distinct work packages — earthwork, base layer, paving, drainage. Each scope gets bid out separately so the right specialists price their part.

3
📨

Contractors receive the invitation to bid

The owner (or a general contractor) sends out a bid package to qualified contractors. This includes the plans, specs, scope of work, and a hard deadline — called bid day. This is how contractors find out the job exists and what they're being asked to price.

4
🏭

Producers quote the material

Contractors call the quarry or plant: "What's your price per ton for base aggregate?" The producer gives them a material price, which feeds directly into the bid. Without this number, contractors can't price their scope.

5
🚛

Haulers quote the haul

Contractors also ask haulers: "What would you charge to move 500 tons from that quarry to our job site?" This is where Tread's quote feature lives. The hauler submits a haul rate — and that rate locks in their margin if the contractor wins.

6
🔍

Contractors assemble and submit their bid

Using material quotes + haul quotes + their own equipment and labor costs, each contractor builds a total price and submits it by bid day. How they submit depends on the project type:

  • Public / government jobs — submitted through an online portal (Bid Express, PlanetBids, eBidUSA). Larger DOT jobs may still require a sealed physical envelope, opened publicly at a formal bid opening.
  • Private projects — usually emailed as a PDF, or submitted through a project management platform the GC runs (Procore, Autodesk Build, BuilderTrend).

Either way, the submission is the bid — a written, binding price offer.

7
🏆

Owner reviews and awards the job

The owner opens all bids, compares prices, and awards each scope to the winning contractor. Contract signed — now the real work begins and trucks can roll.

Tread's quote feature = Step 3.  Haulers respond to contractor requests with formal quotes before the job is even awarded. Multiple contractors can win different scopes on the same project.

Who does what

👷

The Contractor

The Hub of Everything

Won the bid. Manages the whole job. Orders material from the producer and hires the hauler to move it. Every dollar flows through them.

Often a GC who won a public or private bid. On site, the foreman is their representative; for trucking and materials, the hauler is their vendor. The contractor orchestrates both.

💳 Pays for materials AND hauling
🏭

The Producer

Quarry or Plant

A quarry, gravel pit, or asphalt plant. They mine or manufacture the material and sell it to the contractor — priced per ton or cubic yard.

Think of them like a warehouse: they make the product, you come and pick it up.

💵 Gets paid for the material
🚛

The Hauler

Broker or Vendor

The company responsible for getting material from the producer to the job site. In Tread, haulers come in two forms:

  • Vendor — owns trucks and employs drivers directly. They dispatch their own fleet.
  • Broker — doesn't own trucks. They coordinate owner-operators or subcontracted carriers and manage the job administratively.

Either way, they set haul rates during bidding and keep the margin between what they bill the contractor and what they pay the drivers.

💵 Gets paid for the hauling
📡

The Dispatcher

Real-Time Operations

Assigns trucks to jobs in real time. Not exclusive to the hauler — any org that manages a fleet has one. A large producer dispatching its own trucks, a contractor that self-performs hauling, or a dedicated trucking company can all employ dispatchers.

High-tempo, decision-dense role. Which truck goes to which job, how to handle a no-show, when to pull a driver from one job to cover another. They track the fleet live throughout the day.

🎯 Found inside any org that manages a fleet
👤

The Driver

Behind the Wheel

The person physically driving the truck. They work for or are contracted by the hauler — not the contractor directly.

In Tread, the driver is the one who logs loads, captures tickets, and records tons delivered. Their data is what makes everything else work.

💵 Paid by the hauler per load, ton, or hour
📱 Works primarily from the Tread mobile app
🪖

The Foreman

The Contractor's Eyes on Site

Works for the contractor. Manages the physical job site day-to-day — directing workers, coordinating material arrival, and telling trucks exactly where to dump.

When a truck pulls in, the foreman is the point of contact. They inspect the load, sign the ticket, and are the first to know when something goes wrong: short load, wrong material, truck that never arrived.

🏗️ Reports to the contractor; runs the job site
📱 Works primarily from the Tread mobile app

Tread connects all six — the contractor funds the job, the producer supplies the material, the dispatcher assigns the trucks, the driver makes the run, the foreman signs off at the site, and the hauler bills the margin.

05 — THE TRUCKS

Truck choice = trip count = cost

The type of truck directly affects how many trips a job takes. Trips are what cost money — so picking the right truck is a real financial decision.

Same job. Same 500 tons of gravel. Three trucks. Three very different outcomes.
Tandem (small)

3 axles · 16 tons per load

31 trips needed
Tri-Axle (mid)

4 axles · 18 tons per load

28 trips needed
Transfer (large)

5–6 axles · 24 tons per load

21 trips needed

More axles = weight spread over more wheels = government allows a heavier load.

Why not always use the biggest truck?

🚧Tight job sites — big trucks can't fit on narrow or confined sites
⚖️Legal weight limits — some roads and bridges cap axle loads
🏭Loading facilities — some quarries can't load large trucks under their chutes
📦Material type — some materials need specific drop methods (like a belly dump for paving prep)

09 — LOADS & TICKETS

How work gets recorded

Every load has to be tracked and verified. Here's the vocabulary you'll hear every day.

What's a Load?

One complete truck trip: pickup at the source → drive to the job site → dump → return empty. That's one load.

500 ton job ÷ 16 tons per load = 31 loads
Just arithmetic — total ÷ truck capacity = trips needed

Real jobs range from 50 to 50,000+ tons. The math is always the same.

What's a Scale Ticket?

The receipt for each load. The truck is weighed at the source. The scale prints a ticket with three numbers:

Gross weighttruck + material
Tare weightempty truck (pre-registered)
NET weight BILLED actual material

At the job site, the foreman receives each truck and signs off on the ticket — verifying what arrived matches what was ordered. This is the start of every downstream billing and settlement chain.

What if there's no ticket?

More common than you'd think — and Tread handles it.

🌍

Fill dirt and topsoil often skip the scale — no weigh station, billed per load not per ton

📄

Some customers use paper tickets and enter them manually in Tread

📱

Both work — Tread supports auto-capture from photos AND manual entry

Three ways to bill a job

The billing method is agreed upfront. It drives everything that follows.

Per Ton
Scale ticket net weight is the source of truth. Most common method.
Per Load
Trip count is the source of truth. Used when no scale exists.
Per Hour
Time on site is the source of truth. Standby and specialty equipment.

10 — FOLLOW THE MONEY

Four parties. Multiple payments.
One margin in the middle.

The contractor funds it all — paying the producer for material and the hauler for moving it. The hauler keeps the spread between what they bill and what they pay drivers.

👷
The Contractor
The Source
Funds the entire job. Pays the producer for material and the hauler for moving it. Also gets funded themselves by whoever owns the project — a city, developer, or private company.
💸 Paying for the work Margin on hauling: 10–20%
🏭
The Producer
The Supplier
Quarry or plant that mines or makes the material. Invoices the contractor per ton — completely separate from the hauling bill.
💰 Gets paid Margin: 20–40%
🚛
The Hauler (Broker or Vendor)
The Middle
Bills the contractor, pays the driver, keeps the spread. Rate is locked in at bid time — that's how they protect their profit.
💰 Gets paid · Makes money Margin: 15–30%
👤
The Driver
The Executor
Paid by the hauler per load, ton, or hour. Their rate is always lower than what the hauler charges the contractor — that gap is the hauler's profit.
💰 Gets paid
💡  The hauler's entire business model depends on that gap between what the contractor pays them and what they pay the driver. The bigger and more accurate that gap is tracked, the more profitable they are. Tread's settlement tools make sure every driver gets paid the right amount — so the hauler's books close cleanly on every job.
